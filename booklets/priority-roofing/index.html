Episode 1 · A mess of data, everywhereThe before

Priority did not lack software. It had, if anything, too much of it. Over the years the company had run the mainstream roofing platforms — Roof Link, AccuLynx, JobNimbus — and none of them were bad tools. They were simply built for a generic roofing company, and Priority had stopped being generic years ago.

So the work leaked. Quoting lived partly in one system and partly in spreadsheets. Material orders went out by email and came back as phone calls. Delivery confirmations were photos in somebody’s camera roll. Commissions were calculated at month-end from four sources that never quite agreed, and subcontractor payments were chased thread by thread.

At 700 people, email is the least efficient way to run anything. Every branch that grew needed another office admin just to keep work from falling through the cracks — and every new admin had to learn a dozen disconnected tools before they were useful.

3 CRMs
tried and outgrown before Priority built its own

The turning point wasn’t a feature request. It was a staffing realization: growth had a headcount tax. Opening a branch meant hiring coordinators whose whole job was moving information between systems that refused to talk.

Priority’s ask to EphrAIm was blunt — stop bending the company to fit a product. Map how a job actually runs, from the first knock to the last commission check, and build one platform that carries all of it.

“When I used other CRMs, I worked around my problems. Now, with our custom CRM, I can work with our unique workflow through the system — no workarounds.”Greg Johnston · Charlotte branch (on record)

Episode 2 · Nothing falls through anymoreThe office

Every job, one lifecycle

The platform’s spine is a nine-step job lifecycle. It isn’t a suggestion — it’s the rail every job rides, and the reason nothing needs chasing. When a step completes, the next one is already lit.

  1. Job details — documents, notes, requests, invoices
  2. Material order — generated, costed, emailed to the supplier
  3. Production schedule — PM and crew assigned, calendar set
  4. Material verification — delivered vs. ordered, on site
  5. Crew report — labor hours and costs
  6. Final inspection — checklist plus photos of the finished roof
  7. Job completion — certificate for the homeowner
  8. Final commission — calculated from QuickBooks truth
  9. Close-out — the record that ends arguments

Jobs group into three stages — pre-production, production, post-production — and QuickBooks stays the financial source of truth throughout: invoices, payments, expenses, bills.

The board view

For the admin, the kanban board is the branch at a glance: every job a card, every column a state, drag-and-drop as the work moves. Review statuses — submitted, approved, rejected, resubmitted — mean a rep in the field and an admin at a desk are always looking at the same truth.

Episode 2 · Nothing falls through anymoreThe office

Materials without the phone tag

Before: an order was an email to a supplier, a reply-all with a price, a forwarded confirmation, and a hope that what showed up on the driveway matched. The gaps between those messages were where money went to disappear.

Now the order is generated from the job itself — line items, quantities, costs — sent to the supplier from inside the platform, and tracked through pending → generated → delivered. When the truck arrives, the field verifies the delivery against the order, on the job record, with photos. Discrepancies surface the day they happen, not at invoice time.

Episode 2 · Nothing falls through anymoreThe office

The end of commission week

Ask any roofing office what month-end used to mean and you’ll hear the same word: reconciliation. Contract amounts from one system, payments from another, splits negotiated in memory, and a rep in the doorway asking where his check is.

On the platform, commission is step eight of the lifecycle, not a separate ordeal. Each job carries its contract amount, its QuickBooks payments and expenses, its split percentages. Requests move through review states the same way jobs do — pending, processing, ready to finalize, approved — and the 7% draw system lets reps take early draws against commissions without a single side-spreadsheet.

Episode 3 · The day in a pocketThe driveway

The kitchen-table close

The sale itself got its own tool. After a storm, the rep builds a property damage assessment on site — photos, findings, roof-system options — and assembles a proposal from drag-and-drop sections: cover page, damage summary, estimate, photo grid, terms.

The homeowner gets a clean link — Your Property Assessment Report — reviews it at the kitchen table, and signs electronically. No printouts, no “I’ll drop the contract by Thursday.” The proposal that used to take an evening at the office happens before the rep leaves the neighborhood.

Episode 4 · Proof from the rooftopProduction

The middle miles of a job

Production is where roofing jobs traditionally go dark: sold, then silent, then suddenly done — or suddenly a problem. The lifecycle’s middle steps keep the lights on. The PM and crew are assigned on a calendar the whole branch can see. Materials are verified against the order the day they land. The crew report captures labor hours and costs while they’re still fresh.

And through all of it runs PriorityCAM — the company’s own job-site photo system. Crews and PMs capture and annotate photos on the roof; the office organizes them into shareable, printable reports. Delivery shortfalls, mid-roof surprises, finished work: photographed, tagged to the job, arguing with nobody.

Episode 4 · Proof from the rooftopProduction

Paying the people who did the work

The quietest automation in the company may be the one that preps crew pay. Behind the office, a reconciliation engine cross-checks what crews are owed against the evidence — approvals and corrections in the office inbox, tear-off quantities on the production calendar, line-item rates on the payables ledger — and projects a clean review queue for the office each pay cycle.

No one’s check depends on somebody remembering an email. The office reviews exceptions instead of building spreadsheets — deterministic matching does the bookkeeping, humans keep the judgment, and every payment stays human-approved.

The newest member of the finance team isn’t a person. Day 1 — the knock and the yes

8:40 a.m. Hail came through Kessler Parkway on a Tuesday. The rep’s canvassing map has the neighborhood painted by Wednesday; the knock at 2419 goes into the platform as a lead before he’s back at the curb.

6:15 p.m. Assessment built on-site: photos, findings, a retail shingle proposal at $38,450. The homeowner reads it on the customer link and signs at the kitchen table. The rep converts lead to job from the truck and submits. His part of the paperwork is over.

Day 2, 6:50 a.m. The job is in the office admin’s morning queue. Approved by 7:10. Material order generated by 7:30 — supplier emailed from inside the platform, delivery requested for Friday.

Day 4 — the truck arrives

Friday, 8:05 a.m. Delivery hits the driveway; the PM verifies it against the PO on his phone — quantities received, one bundle short, flagged and reordered by 8:20 with the photo attached. The install date sits on the production calendar with a crew assigned; the homeowner never calls to ask, because there’s nothing to ask.

Day 9 — done, documented, paid

Install day. The crew photographs tear-off, decking, and finished ridge in PriorityCAM; the crew report logs hours before the trucks leave. Final inspection checklist, with photos, completes that evening. The homeowner’s completion certificate generates from the job record.

Day 9. The invoice posts to QuickBooks. The payment lands against the job. Commission — a clean 10%, no split — computes from actuals and enters the approval queue. The rep watches it move from his phone; the GM sees the job’s margin land on the branch dashboard the same day; Vera notes the invoice as paid and moves on.

Nobody forwarded anything. Nobody asked where anything was. Quote to close, roughly 75% faster than the company’s old normal — and every one of the 700 people who touched it worked in the same system.

Built for what’s next

The measurable part is easy to say: jobs run in roughly a quarter of the time they used to take. Thirty branches went live on one platform in under a year. Growth stopped requiring a wave of office hires every time a new branch opened — the system does the lift, and headcount goes where it earns.

The cultural part matters more. A company that used to argue about whose spreadsheet was right now argues about what to do next — which is what arguments are for. And the platform keeps growing: an AI copilot inside the CRM that knows every page and every role, Vera on the receivables, more of the manual middle automated every quarter.
