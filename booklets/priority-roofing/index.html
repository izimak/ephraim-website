Part 1 · A growing company had outgrown its systemsThe starting point

By 2024, Priority Roofing employed more than 700 people across 30 branches and completed over $200 million in work a year. The company had adopted three commercial roofing CRMs in succession, and each had been abandoned for the same reason: the software described a generic roofing company, and Priority’s workflow no longer matched it.

The gap between the software and the work was filled by email. Quoting, estimating, material orders, delivery confirmation, photo records, commission calculations, and subcontractor payments each ran partly in a system and partly in inboxes, spreadsheets, and phone calls. At the company’s scale, that arrangement produced two measurable costs.

The first was coordination headcount. Management reports that each new branch required additional office staff whose primary function was moving information between systems, and that training a new administrator meant training them on the full set of disconnected tools at once.

The second was cycle time. Work waited on handoffs, and handoffs waited on people noticing email. The company’s own before-and-after comparison puts the eventual reduction in quote-to-close cycle time at roughly 75 percent, which indicates how much of the original cycle was waiting rather than work.

Part 2 · The officeThe morning review queue

On a typical morning before rollout, a branch administrator began the day by reconstructing the state of the branch: unread email, voicemail, and a set of spreadsheets that disagreed with one another. Under the platform, the day begins with a review queue. Jobs submitted overnight by sales representatives appear on the branch dashboard awaiting approval; the week’s schedule, residential and commercial counts, and resubmissions appear alongside (Exhibit 1). The information the administrator previously assembled by hand is assembled by the system, and the administrator’s first hour is spent on decisions rather than collection.

— 7 —
Part 2 · The officeThe lifecycle

Every job follows the same nine steps

The platform’s central design decision is a fixed job lifecycle. Each job passes through nine steps in order, and completing one step opens the next. The sequence encodes the company’s standard operating procedure, which previously existed as institutional knowledge.

  1. Job details — documents, notes, requests, invoices
  2. Material order — itemized, costed, sent to the supplier
  3. Production schedule — project manager and crew assigned
  4. Material verification — delivered quantities checked against the order
  5. Crew report — labor hours and costs recorded
  6. Final inspection — checklist with photographs
  7. Job completion — certificate issued to the homeowner
  8. Final commission — calculated from QuickBooks records
  9. Close-out — the completed record archived

Jobs group into three stages: pre-production, production, and post-production. QuickBooks remains the financial source of truth for invoices, payments, expenses, and bills throughout.

Review statuses synchronize the field and the office

Each job also carries a review status: submitted, approved, rejected, or resubmitted. A representative in the field and an administrator at a desk see the same status at the same time, which removed the status-inquiry calls that previously occupied both. The board view presents the branch’s pipeline as columns (Exhibit 2).

Part 2 · The officeMaterial orders

Material orders carry their own audit trail

Before the platform, a material order was an email thread: the order itself, the supplier’s reply, a forwarded confirmation, and, at delivery, a phone call if the load was short. Discrepancies were typically discovered at invoice time, when they were most expensive to resolve.

Under the platform, the order is generated from the job record with line items, quantities, and costs; it is sent to the supplier from within the system; and it moves through three states — pending, generated, delivered. When the truck arrives, the field verifies delivered quantities against the order on the job record, with photographs (Exhibit 3). Discrepancies surface on the day of delivery.

Part 2 · The officeCommissions

Commission became a lifecycle step rather than a month-end project

Commission preparation was previously a reconciliation exercise: contract amounts from one system, payments from another, and split arrangements held partly in memory. It consumed office time at every month-end and generated disputes in proportion to its opacity.

On the platform, commission is step eight of the lifecycle. Each job carries its contract amount, its QuickBooks payments and expenses, and its split percentages; requests move through the same review states as jobs (Exhibit 4). A separate draw facility lets representatives request advances against earned commission through the system, with the same approval trail.

Part 3 · The fieldThe mobile workday

A sales representative’s day runs from a vehicle, and the platform was built to be operated from one. The same system the office uses on desktops runs in full on the representative’s phone: leads, jobs, schedule, and commission status, arranged for single-handed use (Exhibits 5 and 6).

The morning view shows active jobs and submissions, the week’s installations, and the branch’s door-knocking leaderboard, which ranks representatives by canvassing activity and recognizes a monthly leader. Management adopted the leaderboard to make prospecting effort visible; participation is reported to be high, though the company has not yet quantified its effect on lead volume.

Each job displays its review status. A representative who wants to know whether a submission was approved checks the status rather than calling the office, and the office is not interrupted to answer.

Part 3 · The fieldProposals at the point of sale

The proposal is produced at the property, not after it

After a storm, a representative documents damage on site and assembles a proposal from standard sections: cover page, findings, estimate, photographs, and terms. The homeowner receives a private link to the assessment, reviews it, and signs electronically. The proposal step that previously required an evening at the office now completes during the visit.

The company has not yet published a measured effect on close rates. The observable change is in cycle: the interval between a homeowner’s verbal agreement and a signed contract, which previously spanned days when contracts traveled by truck, is now bounded by the length of the visit.

Part 3 · The fieldSubmission and handoff

A signed job enters the lifecycle from the driveway

When the homeowner signs, the representative creates the job on the phone — address, property type, roof system, customer details — and submits it for review. From submission onward the lifecycle carries the job: the office approves it in the morning queue, the material order is generated, and the production schedule fills in. The representative’s remaining involvement is monitoring status.

This is the exchange that secured field adoption: several minutes of structured entry at the point of sale, in return for the elimination of evening administrative work and status calls. Representatives who had resisted three previous CRMs adopted this one; the company attributes the difference to the fact that the workflow in the software matches the workflow on the ground.

Part 4 · ProductionScheduling, verification, documentation

The production steps enforce their own records

Lifecycle steps three through seven govern production. The project manager and crew are assigned on a calendar visible to the branch. Delivered materials are verified against the order at the site. The crew report records labor hours and costs on completion, and the final inspection requires a checklist with photographs before the job can proceed to completion.

Photographic documentation runs on PriorityCAM, a companion application built for the company: crews and project managers capture and annotate photographs on the roof, and the office assembles them into shareable reports tied to the job record. The practical effect is that delivery shortfalls, mid-job discoveries, and finished work are documented at the moment they occur, by the person standing in front of them.

Part 4 · ProductionCrew payment

Crew payment preparation moved from reconstruction to review

Paying subcontractor crews correctly requires reconciling three sources: the payables ledger that states what each crew is owed, the correspondence in which approvals and corrections were agreed, and the production calendar that records what work actually occurred. Before automation, the office assembled this reconciliation by hand each pay cycle.

An automated reconciliation engine now performs the matching. It cross-references the three sources, applies the company’s line-item rates, and produces a review queue of exceptions for the office. Matching is deterministic — the same inputs always produce the same result — and every payment retains a human approval. The office’s role in crew pay shifted from assembling the picture to ruling on the cases the engine flags.

Part 5 · ReportingThe executive view

The analytics portal presents revenue, gross margin, EBITDA, and receivables with year-over-year comparisons, and ranks every branch on the same basis (Exhibit 7). The ranking is meaningful because the company first defined profitability centrally — the accounting rules, the benchmarks, and the category assignments — before building the reporting. Under the previous arrangement, each branch tracked performance in its own format, and like-for-like comparison across 30 branches was not practically possible. Reporting now draws from the same QuickBooks records that run the business, so the figures a general manager sees are current rather than assembled after month-end.

— 19 —
Part 5 · ReportingBranch comparison

Two management consequences follow. First, cost is visible at the level of the individual job, so a job running unprofitably can be identified while it is still open rather than in the month after it closes. Second, a general manager and the CFO now review the same figures, defined the same way, which converts performance discussions from disputes over whose numbers are right into decisions about what to do.

Part 5 · ReportingReceivables

Receivables follow-up was assigned to software

The company’s accounts-receivable follow-up now runs through an AI assistant, internally named Vera, that reads open invoices, scores follow-ups by priority, flags jobs missing expected milestones, and drafts follow-up correspondence for staff review. Staff approve and send; the system does not contact customers autonomously.

The design principle matches the rest of the platform: automation performs the monitoring and preparation, and people retain the judgment. The measurable claim — that fewer balances age past the company’s thresholds — is expected but not yet verified; the figure belongs in this section when finance can supply it.

Part 6 · Anatomy of a jobSale through delivery

Days 1–2: contact, contract, approval

Day 1. Following a hailstorm, the canvassing map assigns the affected neighborhood; the knock at 2419 Kessler Parkway is logged as a lead from the representative’s phone. That evening, the representative documents the damage on site, assembles a retail shingle proposal at $38,450, and the homeowner reviews the assessment on the customer link and signs electronically. The representative converts the lead to a job and submits it.

Day 2. The job appears in the branch review queue at the start of business and is approved. The material order is generated from the job record and sent to the supplier within the hour, with delivery requested for day 4.

Day 4: delivery, verified at the site

The delivery is checked against the purchase order at the property. One line item arrives short; the shortfall is recorded on the job with a photograph, and the supplier correction is requested the same morning. The installation date and crew assignment are already on the production calendar, visible to the office, the project manager, and the representative (Exhibit 10).

— 23 —
Part 6 · Anatomy of a jobInstallation through payment

Days 5–9: installation, inspection, invoice, commission

Installation day. The crew documents tear-off, decking condition, and the finished roof in the photo system; the crew report records labor hours before the crew leaves. The final-inspection checklist, with photographs, is completed the same evening, and the homeowner’s completion certificate is generated from the job record.

Day 9. The invoice posts to QuickBooks and the payment is recorded against the job. Commission — 10 percent, no split — is computed from the recorded financials and enters the approval queue, where the representative can watch its status. The job’s margin appears in the branch reporting the same day, and the receivables assistant registers the invoice as paid.

At no point in the nine steps did the job depend on a forwarded email or a status phone call. Under the company’s pre-platform baseline, the same sequence routinely spanned several weeks; the company’s before-and-after comparison across its job portfolio puts the typical cycle-time reduction at roughly 75 percent.

What changed, and what the next phase requires

The measured results are stated above: job cycle times reduced by roughly 75 percent by the company’s own comparison, 30 branches consolidated onto one platform in under a year, and branch growth decoupled from coordination hiring. The organizational result is harder to quantify but consistently reported: disagreements about whose figures are correct have largely disappeared, because the figures have one source.

The next phase is underway. An AI assistant embedded in the CRM answers role- and page-specific questions for staff; the receivables assistant is extending across branches; and the company continues to move manual processes onto the platform as they are documented. Each extension follows the pattern established here: encode the company’s own procedure, automate the monitoring, and keep approvals with people.
